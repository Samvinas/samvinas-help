# Facilitating the room

Facilitation has a large literature, but most of the weight is carried by a
handful of habits. Here are the six we keep returning to — in a Samvinas
event, or any meeting at all. The bold line in each section is the habit;
the rest is the reasoning, there when you want it.

## Guard the process; stay out of the content

**The problem belongs to the group; the way of working belongs to you.** Your
authority as a facilitator rests on that division, and it is surprisingly easy
to give away. The moment you champion an idea, or even reword one, you have
quietly become a judge — and once the group has seen you judge, deferred
judgement stops being credible.

Sometimes an idea of your own simply won't let go. When that happens, say
plainly that you are stepping out of the facilitator role, offer the idea once
without selling it, and step back in.

## Guide by the side, not sage on the stage

The temptation is to be the expert in the room. Resist it: **what the group
needs from you is structure, not answers.** Ask the question, set up the
activity, and let the room do the thinking. Conclusions a group reaches for
itself are the only ones it keeps — hand people yours and they will nod
politely, then forget. (The rhyme is owed to the educator Alison King.)

## Neutral doesn't mean passive

**Staying out of the content is not fading into the wallpaper.** A neutral
facilitator still runs the room, and runs it actively — on the group's behalf
rather than on any argument's. Protect the quieter voices, introduce the
strangers, and make it plain that status stays at the door: in here, the
intern's idea weighs the same as the director's. Saying such rules out loud
can feel heavy-handed, but the effect is the opposite — clear ground rules
make people freer, not less free.

## Make participation full, not merely open

An open floor sounds fair, but **"anyone can speak" quietly becomes "the
confident speak."** And there is a structural problem as well as a social one:
in open discussion only one person can talk at a time, so everyone else's
thinking stalls while they wait their turn. Researchers call this **production
blocking**, and it is exactly why every Samvinas generating tool lets the
whole room write at once.

You can do the same work in the room itself. Start topics with a few minutes
of silent writing, invite quieter people by name — with a free pass to
decline — and when one view is gathering momentum, ask who sees it
differently.

## Respect different ways of working

**Some people talk in order to think; others think before they will talk.**
A group needs both, but a session built entirely around quick spoken exchange
only ever hears from the first kind. So alternate solo, pair and whole-group
moments; share the challenge ahead of the day, so the reflectors arrive with
their thinking already begun; and after you ask a question, hold a few
seconds of quiet. Silence is thinking, not failure.

## Keep the ground rules, and the group's words

Two things are in your keeping for the whole event. The first is the ground
rules: you hold the group to
[deferring judgement](/principles/deferring-judgement.html) while the field
is open, and to [affirmative-first choosing](/principles/convergence.html)
when it narrows.

The second is the group's language. **Capture ideas in the contributor's own
words** — rewording is judgement in disguise, however helpful it feels in the
moment. If an idea runs long, ask its owner for the headline rather than
writing one for them.

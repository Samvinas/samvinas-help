# Evaluation Matrix — for facilitators

The Evaluation Matrix is a **convergent** tool that compares a set of options
against several **criteria** at once. Each option gets a score on every
criterion, so you can see not just which option is strongest overall but *why* —
where each one is strong or weak.

## Samvinas

- Pipe in the options to evaluate (for example, the shortlist from a vote).
- In the tool's settings, list your **criteria** (comma- or line-separated), e.g.
  "Impact, Effort, Fit". Set the **rating scale** maximum if 1–5 isn't right.
- Set the criteria *before* scoring starts — changing them mid-way detaches
  earlier scores.
- Participants rate **one criterion at a time**: all options on the first
  criterion, then "Next →" to the second, and so on. Short screens, and everyone
  uses the same yardstick for each criterion.

The results matrix shows each option's average on every criterion, a **Total**
(the mean of its criterion averages), and how many people scored it. A small
"±" is the spread — a large spread means the group disagreed on that cell,
which is often the most interesting thing to discuss.

![The Evaluation Matrix results grid: four options as rows, the criteria Impact, Effort and Cost as columns, with a mean and a plus-or-minus spread in each cell. A Total column ranks the options, and an n column shows how many people scored.](/assets/images/screenshots/evaluation-matrix.png)

The matrix consumes items from an earlier tool — connect its **Source** to the
vote or list that produced the shortlist.

## Paper and pen

You need: a flip chart with the grid drawn large — options as rows, criteria as
columns, plus a Total column — and a scoring sheet (the same grid, small) per
person.

- Agree the criteria first and write them as column headings, phrased so
  **higher always means better**.
- Everyone fills their own sheet, working **column by column** — score every
  option on the first criterion before moving to the next, so each criterion is
  judged on one yardstick.
- Collect the sheets; two people tally averages into the wall grid while the
  group takes a break. Circle any cell where individual scores were far apart.
- One failure mode to avoid: mixed-polarity criteria ("Impact" high-is-good
  next to "Cost" high-is-bad) — the totals become meaningless. Rephrase to
  "Affordability" before anyone scores.
- Harvest: photograph the wall grid, circled cells and all, and note the top
  two or three totals on a fresh sheet.

## When & why

Reach for the Evaluation Matrix late in convergence, when the shortlist is down
to a handful of serious options and the decision has several dimensions that
deserve separate scrutiny.

> A [convergent](/principles/convergence.html) step. Its purpose isn't to crown
> a single winner — use it to understand the field, then combine, modify, or
> sequence the strongest options.

- Best with a handful to a dozen options and a few clear criteria.
- Follow the matrix with a discussion of the high-spread cells — that's where
  the group is scoring on different assumptions.
- What usually follows: [PPCO](/facilitator/ppco.html) or
  [ALoU](/facilitator/alou.html) to strengthen a leading option, or
  [Elaboration](/facilitator/elaboration.html) to develop it in depth.
